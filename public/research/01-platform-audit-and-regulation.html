Gambling licences held by the three operators, so none is bound by any RNG or disclosure standard
  1. All three sites use the same odds engine. A weighted prize table is mapped onto a uniform random roll from a random number generator. The roll comes from a commit-reveal ("provably fair") scheme: the hash of the server seed is published first, the player's client seed and a counter (the nonce) are mixed in with HMAC, and the output is scaled into [0,1) or [0,100).
  2. Provably fair verifies the roll, not the odds. The hash check proves the server seed was fixed before play. It does not prove the prize table was fixed, that the table shown is the table used, that the item "values" are real, or that the entropy was not chosen by the operator. Every documented rigging case sits in those gaps, not in the hash.
  3. The published numbers are internally consistent and, at face value, honest. Cases.gg discloses a 10% house edge and its Pack Up box computes to 89.5% return at its own valuations. Jemlit's Apple eBox computes to an expected value of $6.78 at listed prices. Misty's boxes return 37 to 41% of price in physical goods plus "credits". The deception is in presentation: one-in-a-million showcase items, credits valued at face, valuations the site itself sets, and a verification path almost no player exercises.
  4. None of the three holds a gambling licence. Misty relies on the UK free-draw and prize-competition exemptions. Jemlit (Cyprus company, UK governing law) states no licence. Cases.gg (Cyprus company, Delaware payment entity) relies on a US sweepstakes "No purchase necessary" structure. So none is bound by the RNG and disclosure standards that licensed operators must meet.
  5. Regulation is moving but fragmented. New York sued Valve on 25 February 2026 calling loot boxes "quintessential gambling". Ten US states banned dual-currency sweepstakes casinos in 2025 and 2026. GLI published a draft GLI-19 v4.0 (7 July 2026) that for the first time specifies requirements for provably-fair RNGs. The EU Digital Fairness Act proposal is expected in Q4 2026. The UK still does not classify loot boxes as gambling.

1 · How the odds engine works

1.1 The generic algorithm

When an admin "sets a 0.5% chance", this is what the code does. Each item owns a slice of the unit interval whose width is its weight; a uniform roll from the RNG picks the slice it lands in.

table = [(item_1, w_1), (item_2, w_2), ..., (item_n, w_n)]   # weights sum to 1 (or 100)
cumulative ranges: item_k owns (sum_{i<k} w_i , sum_{i<=k} w_i]
roll r ~ Uniform[0, 1)
winner = the item whose range contains r

Everything about fairness reduces to two questions: is the roll uniform and fixed before the outcome is known, and is the table that was used the table that was shown.

1.2 The commit-reveal ("provably fair") layer

server_seed   : secret random string generated by the operator
commitment    : H(server_seed) published BEFORE play   (SHA-256 on Cases.gg and Misty, SHA-512 on Jemlit)
client_seed   : string chosen by, or generated for, the player
nonce         : counter 1, 2, 3 ... per bet under the same server seed
roll          : HMAC(key = server_seed, msg = client_seed || nonce)  ->  hex  ->  integer  ->  scale to [0,1)
reveal        : when the server seed is "retired" it is published; anyone can check H(server_seed) == commitment
                and recompute every roll made under it

In plain words. Think of the server seed as the winning numbers written on a card and sealed in an envelope. The hash is the wax seal: the site shows it to you before you pay, and the seal would look completely different if a single digit on the card were changed. Your client seed is a second card you hand over, so the site cannot have pre-chosen the result from its card alone. The nonce just numbers your bets so each one gets a fresh roll from the same two cards. After you play, the envelope is opened: you hash the revealed seed yourself and compare it with the seal. If it matches, the site could not have swapped its card after seeing your bet.

What the seal does not tell you is what prizes were on the shelf, what they were really worth, or whether the site tried a thousand cards in private before choosing which one to seal. Those are the gaps in section 3.

const key = ["DRAW", slug, serverSeed, clientSeed, entryCount].join("-");

const value = parseInt(hmac.slice(0, 13), 16);

Finding, revised 10 Oct. The snippet as published never feeds a message into the HMAC: it computes an HMAC over an empty message keyed by the combined string. That is unusual, but Packs.com documents exactly the same convention (section 2.5), so the snippet is deterministic and reproducible once a seed is revealed. tools/verify_roll.py misty computes the as-published form, which is the one to trust. The remaining gaps are that the box-opening formula, as opposed to the draw formula, is not published, and that no draw had revealed a seed at capture, so nothing could yet be checked end to end.

The credits layer is where the edge hides. Credits are presented as entries "from $1", so nominally the $10 box returns $3.77 in goods plus $14 in credits, a 178% return. But a credit is a ticket in a draw whose expected value depends on sell-through: if the 100,000-ticket pool sells out at about $1, one ticket is worth $0.32, and the box's real return is about 82% for the Timepiece Suite and 68% for the Penthouse. What this adds to the first audit.

1. Still nobody commits the drop table or the valuations. The best current practice is an external randomness beacon (the EOS block hash at Razed, PackDraw and Chicken.gg) plus per-open seed rotation (Packs.com). The Glass Box should adopt both on top of the manifest commitment.

2. IcyBox's finite boxes come with an operator exit: a box can be retired before its grail is distributed. A committed pool must bind the operator to distribute the grail, or draw it among the buyers, at retirement.

3. The two trusted brands use no cryptography at all. StockX and Courtyard substitute their own data and, at Courtyard, a public log. Buyback is credit at StockX and fair market value at Courtyard with a 7-day window, against StockX's 5 minutes and IcyBox's forced auto-buyback. A 7-day decision window and cash are differentiating on their own.

4. PullValue shows the comparison layer has demand: an independent expected-value and observed-odds tracker already exists for Courtyard alone.

5. Correction to section 1.3: Misty's draw verifier follows the same empty-message HMAC convention Packs.com documents, so it is reproducible; "defective" was too strong. The unpublished part is the box formula.

3 · Where the loophole actually is

What a passing hash check proves: the server seed existed before the bet, and the roll is a deterministic function of (server seed, client seed, nonce). What it cannot prove, and where every documented abuse has lived:

  1. The weight table is not committed. Only the seed is hashed. An operator can run a different table than the one displayed, or change ranges per account, and every verification still passes. Misty says it "freezes" the table onto each open, which is the right idea, but there is no published hash of the table to check against.
  2. Operator-controlled entropy. If the client seed is server-generated (the default on all three sites until the player changes it), the operator can grind seed pairs offline and keep house-favourable ones. Audit finding Zealynx F-2026-0003 (Fair Casino, January 2026, severity HIGH) documents exactly this; F-2026-0004 documents the server choosing its seed after seeing the client seed. Both passed public verification until fixed.
  3. Seed-rotation timing and aborted rounds. Rotating or discarding seeds at times correlated with wins biases outcomes without touching any individual verifiable roll. UK RTS 7B explicitly forbids discarding RNG output.
  4. Per-account flags. CSGO Diamonds admitted in 2016 that it gave sponsored streamer m0E advance knowledge of roll outcomes. Leaked CSGOShuffle logs (2016) showed the apparent owner asking the coder for roll percentages. A former case-site coder told Richard Lewis in 2017 that win rates are set manually, that sites run a mode paying the two cheapest skins every time, that weights can be entered then erased so they cannot be traced, and that advertised items are configured so they cannot drop: "Every site I've worked on does it this way and requests these features."
  5. The valuation layer. Return is computed against prices the operator sets. Cash-out is in credits at a haircut (Jemlit, 0 to 30%), in competition entries (Misty), or at an unpublished sell rate (Cases.gg).
  6. Presentation. One-in-a-million showcase items; "top open item $159" banners; spin animations with near-miss placement, which New York's complaint against Valve makes a pleaded fact; "40% Guaranteed" boxes; odds that sum to more than 100%.
  7. Detectability. An individual cannot statistically distinguish a 0.26% rate from a 0.18% rate without tens of thousands of paid opens (to be quantified in the simulation phase). Verification requires retiring the seed and running code on a third-party site; in practice almost nobody does it, and the sites know it.

Implication for the thesis. "All online gambling apps are rigged" is not provable and is likely false in the narrow sense of the roll. What is demonstrable, with these sites' own data, is that the design is negative-EV by construction and the edge is disguised through valuation and credits; that the "provably fair" guarantee is narrower than it is marketed to be and has been bypassed in documented cases through the gaps above; and that no independent party has audited any of these three operators against any standard. That is a stronger and more defensible claim than "the RNG is fake".

Operator's-eye confirmation, added 10 Oct 2026. A B2B software vendor's sales demo (dossier 2 §3.5) describes the same mechanics from the inside: 99.9% of pulls are sold back because "the economy is made in a way that most of the time you're getting something that is not really desirable"; withdrawals are allowed only "through items" after wagering; bots let a player "play against the house" in battles; RTP is defaulted to 90% and configurable; item prices are listed above acquisition cost. It is the clearest statement of design intent in this research and belongs in the thesis as primary evidence.

4 · Regulation and standards

4.1 United States

  • Federal. No statute or FTC rule covers loot boxes or mystery boxes. The FTC held "Inside the Game" on 7 August 2019 and issued a Staff Perspective paper in August 2020 calling for meaningful disclosures, research and self-regulation; panelists flagged dynamic odds that vary by player or time as a transparency concern. Deceptive odds claims fall under FTC Act §5. The FTC's 2022 report "Bringing Dark Patterns to Light" reaches gaming.
  • State gambling law decides. Washington's definition (RCW 9.46.0237): risking something of value on a contest of chance with an understanding that something of value will be received on a given outcome. The Washington State Gambling Commission sent Valve a cease-and-desist on 27 September 2016 over skins gambling.
  • New York v. Valve Corp. (Sup. Ct. N.Y. County, Index 450952/2026, filed 25 February 2026). Theory: "charging an individual for a chance to win something of value based on luck alone is quintessential gambling". It pleads Valve's disclosed case odds (Mil-Spec 79.92%, Restricted 15.98%, Classified 3.2%, Covert 0.64%, Exceedingly Rare 0.26%), the slot-like wheel and "near miss", the resale market ($4.3bn in skins), harm to minors, and seeks an injunction, disgorgement, restitution and treble penalties. The theory transfers directly to mystery-box sites with resale or cash-out.
  • Sweepstakes casino bans 2025 to 2026, the structure Cases.gg uses: NJ A5447 (15 Aug 2025), CT PA 25-112 (effective 1 Oct 2025), MT SB 555 (1 Oct 2025), NV SB 256 (1 Oct 2025), NY S5935A (5 Dec 2025, fines $10k to $100k per violation), then 2026 laws in IN (HB 1052), IA (SF 2289), LA (HB 53 and HB 883), ME (LD 2007), OK (SB 1589) and TN (HB 1885). Most are drafted around dual-currency systems that simulate casino games; physical-goods mystery boxes are a grey zone that a court applying the New York theory could close.
  • Sweepstakes law baseline a "No purchase necessary" site must meet: a free entry route with equal odds; official rules disclosing sponsor, eligibility, prize value, odds and winner selection; New York and Florida registration and a bond when total prizes exceed $5,000; Rhode Island registration above $500 for retail promotions.

4.2 Technical standards a licensed operator must meet

  • GLI-19 v3.0 (17 July 2020). RNG seeding and re-seeding rules, no cycling or synchronisation, statistical testing (chi-square, Kolmogorov-Smirnov, DIEHARD-style batteries), background cycling, scaling that preserves uniformity.
  • GLI-19 v4.0 draft (7 July 2026), new §5.5 "Provably Fair" RNGs. The server seed must be committed by hash before wager acceptance and never modified afterwards. The client seed must be provided by the player or generated on the player's device and not be modifiable by the platform. A nonce or counter is required where one seed covers many games. The commitment method, seed combination, every cryptographic primitive, all scaling and shuffling, and the conversion of intermediate values into outcomes must be documented "sufficiently detailed to enable an independent third party to reproduce and validate historical game outcomes". Device-generated hashes may be supplemental entropy but "not the sole entropy source". Measured against this draft: Jemlit documents its algorithm fully; Cases.gg does not document the outcome function; Misty documents the draw but not the box.
  • UK Gambling Commission technical standards. RTS 7A: outcomes must be "acceptably random", demonstrable by statistical testing; adaptive or compensated games are prohibited. RTS 7B: implement the rules "as described to the customer"; random numbers must be used in order and never discarded. RTS 7C: no designs that mislead about likelihood and no false near-miss displays. RTS 3C: before committing, the customer must be able to see how winners are determined and the house edge, the return to player, or the probability of each outcome.
  • Belgian Gaming Commission (April 2018) recommended, beyond disclosure, that the regulator be given control over the random number generators and that spending ceilings be introduced.

4.3 United Kingdom

  • Loot boxes are not gambling under the Gambling Act 2005 (DCMS response 2022; White Paper 2023; 2024 follow-up). Industry self-regulation and the PEGI label since April 2020. Xiao, Henderson and Newall (PLOS ONE, 2023) found 64% compliance with the UK's voluntary odds disclosure versus 95.6% under China's legal rule.
  • Free draws and prize competitions (Schedules 1 and 2): the free route must be as prominent and convenient as the paid route and prizes must be allocated identically; products priced "well above" cost may be challenged as illegal lotteries. The Commission logged 549 complaints about 386 entities between January 2019 and April 2023 and said the subjective tests make prosecution difficult. A DCMS voluntary code for prize draws was due in November 2025.
  • ASA rulings: EA, Miniclip and Jagex (20 March 2024) for ads omitting loot boxes; Hutch Games / F1 Clash (19 November 2025) for a spin wheel that did not make clear prizes had unequal odds; three further app rulings in 2025 following an academic complaint.
  • DCMS "Rapid evidence review of skins gambling" (Harris, Griffiths and Gibson, Nottingham Trent University, published 25 September 2025): 54 sites identified, 6.9 million unique visits in February 2025, 44.9% of visitors aged 18 to 24; none of 20 audited sites verified age at the point of use; near-miss, losses disguised as wins, autoplay and free demo spins found across sites; only one site offered deposit limits. It recommends treating skins gambling as gambling and licensing operators, and describes case openings as simulating a slot machine.
  • The Commission said in 2019 it was "assessing" MysteryBrand, the Jake Paul and RiceGum promotion case with non-delivered and counterfeit prizes. No public outcome was found.

4.4 EU and elsewhere

  • Belgium (2018): paid loot boxes in FIFA 18, CS:GO and Overwatch are games of chance; operators were told to remove them or face prosecution. Netherlands: the 2018 KSA ruling against EA was overturned by the Council of State in 2022.
  • EU Digital Fairness Act: proposal expected Q4 2026 under the Commission's 2026 work programme. The Parliament's IMCO committee (late 2025) asked for a ban on loot boxes and pay-to-win mechanics in games likely to be accessed by minors; odds disclosure is the most consistently expected element. Not adopted as of 9 October 2026.
  • Denmark: HypeDrop reported geo-blocked after the Danish Gambling Authority declined to accept the model (secondary source). China: mandatory probability disclosure since May 2017; a 2024 police case treated live-streamed mystery boxes as gambling. Brazil: paid loot boxes banned for under-18s from March 2026. Australia: chance-based purchases rated 15+ (secondary source). Curaçao: the master-licence and sub-licence regime ended in 2024, so "8048/JAZ" claims are obsolete.

Glossary in plain English

Every dotted term on this page points here. Definitions are written for a reader with no casino, cryptography or legal background.

Randomness and fairness

Random number generator (RNG)
The piece of software that produces the dice roll for every box opening. Computers cannot be truly random, so an RNG starts from a seed and runs a formula that produces numbers that look random. If the formula is good, nobody can predict the next number without knowing the seed.
Seed
The starting value an RNG begins from. The same seed always produces the same sequence of numbers, the way the same shuffle always deals the same hands. That is why seeds matter: whoever controls the seed, and when it is chosen, controls the outcome. Provably fair systems split the seed into a server seed and a client seed so neither side controls it alone.
Server seed
The site's half of the seed: a long secret random string. The site must pick it before you play and must not change it afterwards. It stays secret until it is revealed, so you cannot predict outcomes, but its fingerprint (hash) is shown up front so the site cannot swap it later. "Server seed committed by SHA-256" means that fingerprint was published before you bet.
Client seed
Your half of the seed: a string generated in your browser that you can change at any time. Because the site cannot know it in advance, it cannot pre-cook outcomes from its own seed alone. If you leave the site's default in place, you are trusting that the site chose it honestly.
Nonce
A counter: 1 for your first bet under a seed, 2 for the second, and so on. It makes each roll different even though the two seeds stay the same, the way the same deck deals different hands as you go through it.
Hash (SHA-256, SHA-512)
A fingerprint of a piece of data. Feed any text into SHA-256 and you get a fixed-length code. Change one character and the code changes completely, and you cannot work backwards from the code to the text. It is not encryption, because nothing is hidden to be unlocked later. It is a tamper-evident fingerprint. SHA-512 is the same idea with a longer code.
Commitment (commit-reveal)
The sealed-envelope trick. Before you play, the site shows you the hash of its server seed, which is like showing you a sealed envelope. After you play, it opens the envelope by revealing the seed. You hash the revealed seed yourself; if it matches the fingerprint shown earlier, the seed was not changed in between.
HMAC
A way of hashing a message together with a secret key, so the result depends on both. Here the server seed is the key and your client seed plus the nonce is the message. The output looks random and is turned into the roll. Anyone holding the revealed seed can recompute it, which is what makes verification possible.
Entropy
Genuine unpredictability. A seed with lots of entropy could have been any of a huge number of values; a seed chosen from a short list has little. When the site generates both seeds itself, it controls all the entropy, and "random" becomes "whatever the site picked".
Provably fair
The marketing name for the commit-reveal scheme. It lets you prove, after the fact, that a specific roll was computed from seeds fixed before you bet. It proves nothing about the odds table, the item values, or whether the site picked favourable seeds for itself, which is why this dossier treats it as narrower than it sounds.
Seed rotation, retiring a seed
Replacing the server seed with a new one. The old seed is then revealed so you can check your past bets. Until a seed is retired you cannot verify anything played under it, and the site decides when rotation happens.
Seed grinding
An attack where whoever controls both seeds tries thousands of candidate seeds in private, keeps the one that produces the result they want, and only then "commits" to it. Verification still passes because the commitment was real; it was just chosen with the outcome already known.
Verification tool
A snippet of code the site publishes so you can recompute a roll from the revealed seeds. If the snippet is wrong or missing, you cannot check anything even when the seeds are revealed.
Hexadecimal (hex)
Base-16 numbers written with the digits 0 to 9 and the letters a to f. Hashes are written this way. "First 13 hex characters" means taking the first 13 of those symbols and reading them as a number.

Odds and money

Odds, probability, drop rate
Three names for the same thing: how often an outcome happens over many tries. 0.5% means 5 times in 1,000 opens on average. "1 in 200" is the same number written as a ratio. A low rate does not mean it will take you that many opens; it is an average over a very large number of players, and most of them never see it.
Drop table (weight table, prize table)
The list of everything a box can pay, each with its chance. The chances must add up to 100%. The "admin sets 0.5%" step is editing this table. A site can show one table and run another unless the table itself is locked into the commitment, and on these sites it is not.
Expected value (EV)
The average payout per open if you opened the box a huge number of times: each item's value multiplied by its chance, all added up. A $10 box with an EV of $8.20 loses $1.80 per open on average, however lucky any single open is.
Return to player (RTP)
Expected value as a percentage of the price. 89.5% RTP means that for every $100 spent, players as a group get back $89.50 worth of items at the site's valuations. Casinos publish RTP for slot machines, and licensed online operators must publish it too.
House edge
The flip side of RTP: the share the site keeps on average, which is 100% minus RTP. A 10% house edge on a $1,814 box is $181 per open, expected.
Listed value, valuation layer
The price a site assigns to each prize. RTP is calculated against these prices, so inflating them makes a box look better than it is. The honest test is what you can actually get: the cash-out credit, the resale price, or the raffle ticket's real worth.
Cash-out, cashback credits, Quick Sell, haircut
Ways to turn a won item into site balance instead of shipping it. The haircut is the percentage the site keeps on that conversion (Jemlit: 0 to 30%). Balance usually cannot be withdrawn as money, so it can only be spent on more boxes.
Showcase (headline) items
The expensive prizes shown in the marketing. On these sites they sit at odds like 1 in 1,000,000, so they contribute almost nothing to the expected value while doing most of the selling.
Logarithmic scale
A chart axis where each step is ten times the last (100, 1,000, 10,000 and so on). It is the only way to show 1 in 200 and 1 in 1,000,000 on the same chart without the first one becoming invisible.
Detectability (statistical power)
How many opens you would need before a shaved drop rate shows up as more than bad luck. For rare items the answer is tens of thousands of paid opens, which is why an individual player cannot catch rigging and only a regulator with the logs can.

Game design and psychology

Near miss
When the spinning wheel stops just next to the big prize. It feels like you almost won, but the outcome was decided by the RNG before the animation started, so the placement is theatre. Licensed operators are forbidden from faking it.
Losses disguised as wins
Getting back less than you paid while the game celebrates with lights and sounds, for example a $4 item from a $10 box with a "You won!" animation.
Variable-ratio reinforcement
The reward schedule behind slot machines and loot boxes: wins arrive after an unpredictable number of tries, which produces the most persistent behaviour of any schedule psychologists have tested. Skinner found it in pigeons before casinos found it in people.
Compensated (adaptive) game
A game that quietly changes the odds depending on what happened before, for example tightening after a big win or loosening for a new account. Banned under UK standards because the displayed odds would no longer be true for you.
Structural characteristics
The design features of a gambling product that make it more or less harmful: speed of play, bet size, near-misses, sound and light feedback, how easy it is to bet again. The term comes from Griffiths and Parke.

Site mechanics

Loot box, mystery box, case
A purchase whose contents are random. Loot boxes live inside video games; mystery boxes and cases are sold on standalone websites for real goods or game items. Mechanically they are identical.
Skins
Cosmetic items for games such as Counter-Strike that can be traded and sold for real money. They are the chips of skins gambling: won on third-party sites and converted to cash on marketplaces.
Battles, Upgrader, Crash, Coinflip
Other game modes on these sites. In a battle, two or more players open the same boxes and the highest total takes everything. Upgrader lets you stake an item for a chance at a pricier one. Crash is a rising multiplier you must cash out before it collapses. Coinflip is a 50/50 bet on items. All carry the site's edge.
Live drops feed
The scrolling list of recent wins on the homepage. It is real data, but it shows what people win, not what they paid, and sites choose which wins to feature.
Affiliate site
A review or ranking website paid a commission when readers sign up through its links. Its "best mystery box sites" lists are advertising, which is why this dossier does not treat them as evidence.
Geo-block
Refusing visitors from a country, usually by IP address, so the site does not have to comply with that country's gambling law.

Law and regulation

Gambling licence
Permission from a regulator (the UK Gambling Commission, a US state, Malta and others) to offer gambling. It comes with mandatory RNG testing, published RTP, age checks and deposit limits. None of the three sites has one, so none of those duties applies to them.
Independent audit, RNG certification
A testing lab (GLI, iTech Labs, eCOGRA) checking that an operator's RNG is random and that real payouts match the published odds. Licensed operators must have it. None of these three sites has been audited by anyone.
GLI-19
The most widely used technical standard for online gambling systems, written by Gaming Laboratories International. Version 4.0, in draft since July 2026, is the first to spell out what a provably-fair system must do to pass.
UK RTS
The Gambling Commission's Remote gambling and software Technical Standards, which every UK-licensed operator must follow. RTS 7 covers randomness; RTS 3 covers telling customers their chances.
Sweepstakes, "No purchase necessary", AMOE
In US law a promotion is not gambling if you can enter without paying. Sites offer an alternative method of entry (AMOE), usually a free box or a posted form, so the paid boxes can be framed as a promotion rather than a bet. Several states decided in 2025 and 2026 that the framing fails when the product simulates a casino.
Dual-currency sweepstakes
The sweepstakes-casino model: you buy "gold coins" that cannot be cashed out and receive free "sweeps coins" that can. The 2025 and 2026 state bans are written around this structure.
Free draw, prize competition, free entry route (UK)
The UK exemptions Misty relies on. A draw is not a lottery if there is a free way to enter that is as easy and prominent as paying; a competition is not gambling if it needs skill. The postal entry route and the skill question exist to satisfy those two tests.
Skill question
A simple question, often trivially easy, that entrants must answer so a prize draw can be called a competition of skill rather than a lottery.
Cease and desist
A formal letter from a regulator or lawyer demanding that an activity stop, with legal action threatened if it does not.
Disgorgement, restitution, treble damages
Remedies a court can order. Disgorgement makes the company hand over the profits it made; restitution pays money back to the people who lost it; treble damages multiply the penalty by three.
PGSI
The Problem Gambling Severity Index, a nine-question survey. A score of 8 or more indicates problem gambling. The esports bettors in the literature averaged 10.03.
