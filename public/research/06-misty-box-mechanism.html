Misty fixes the price of a box first, gives every possible prize a chance so that all the chances add up to 100, and pays out whatever the roll lands on. Two things stand out. The $10 boxes pay back about two thirds of the price on paper; the $25 and $50 boxes a little over half. The Handbag Suite, the one with a Hermès Kelly and a Chanel Classic Flap at the top, is the stingiest box on the site: on paper it returns a quarter of the price.

2. One open, step by step

  1. You pick a box and see the price. Say the Timepiece Suite at $10.
  2. Before you pay, the site locks in a secret number for your open. It shows you a scrambled copy of it. The secret cannot be changed later without the copy giving it away.
  3. You can type a seed of your own. Any word. It goes into the roll, so the site cannot pick the roll alone.
  4. You pay and tap to spin. The secret, your seed and the spin number (1 for your first spin under that secret) are mixed together by a fixed recipe into one long number.
  5. The long number becomes a roll between 0 and 100. Only the first 13 characters are used.
  6. The roll is read against the odds table. Think of a line from 0 to 100 cut into bands, one band per outcome, each as wide as that outcome's chance. The roll falls in one band. That band is what you get.
  7. If the outcome carries free spins, they run now. Same secret, spin numbers 2 and 3, read against a small second table that only pays Mistees.
  8. You are paid. A physical piece can be shipped, or traded in for Mistees. Everything else is Mistees, added to your balance.
  9. The secret is revealed. It must match the scrambled copy from step 2, and with it anyone can redo steps 4 to 7 and get the same result. The odds table used for your open is frozen with it.
  10. A new secret is committed for your next open. Nothing from this open carries over.

That is the whole mechanism. There is no pool of prizes that runs out, no counter of losses that makes a win more likely, and no target the system steers toward.

3. A very simple example

Imagine a box called the Little Box. It costs $10. Its odds table has three rows:

What you can getWorthChanceBand on the 0 to 100 line
A watch$4001%0 to 1
A perfume$504%1 to 5
2 Mistees$2 at face95%5 to 100

The chances add up to 100. Now two players open it.

  • Anna's roll is 37.2. That falls in the band from 5 to 100. Anna gets 2 Mistees.
  • Ben's roll is 0.4. That falls in the band from 0 to 1. Ben gets the watch.

Neither roll had anything to do with the other. If a hundred people open the Little Box, the box does not owe anyone a watch. The most likely picture is one watch, four perfumes and ninety-five people with 2 Mistees, but in about one group of three nobody gets the watch at all, and in about one group of four two or more people do.

What the Little Box costs its owner, per open. Take each row's worth, multiply by its chance, add up.

RowWorth × chanceCost per open
Watch$400 × 1%$4.00
Perfume$50 × 4%$2.00
2 Mistees$2 × 95%$1.90
Total paid back per open$7.90
Price$10.00
Owner keeps, on paper$2.10

So the Little Box pays back 79% of its price on paper. Nothing about a single open tells you that: Anna got $2 back and Ben got $400. The 79% only appears when you add up thousands of opens.

The same sum in real money. Mistees only buy more boxes, so they cost the owner far less than $1 (section 6 says about 37 cents). The watch and the perfume can be bought in for roughly 70% of their listed price. Redo the table with those numbers and the box pays back about $4.90, which is 49%. The owner keeps about $5.10 of every $10.

A free spin pays 1 Mistee 70% of the time, 2 Mistees 22%, 5 Mistees 6%, 10 Mistees 2% and 20 Mistees 0.2%, which is 1.68 Mistees on average. The two free-spin rows are valued with that included.

5. Why the box costs $10

The price is not worked out from the contents. The price comes first, and the chances are tuned until the contents fit under it with profit to spare. Three signs point that way:

  • Prices are round tiers. Every box is $10, $25 or $50. A price computed from the contents would land on odd numbers.
  • The Mistee rows are almost the same in every box of a tier. The Vanity, also $10, uses nearly the same Mistee chances as the Timepiece Suite. Only the pieces on top change.
  • The rare piece is the adjustable part. Its chance is the knob that makes a big headline piece fit a small price.

Here is the knob on the Timepiece Suite. Only the Rolex's chance changes; everything else stays as it is.

So why $10? Because it is a price people tap without thinking, and tap again. The Rolex is the headline, and its chance is set exactly as rare as a $16,035 watch needs to be to live inside a $10 box.

6. What a Mistee is worth

Misty's terms say Mistees are site credit with no cash value, that every box pays them, that they never expire, and that they can only be spent on boxes and products on the site. They cannot be withdrawn, transferred or exchanged for money.

The page never says in words what one Mistee is worth. But the data behind the page stores every Mistee outcome at 100 cents per Mistee, and the competitions sell entries from $1. So Misty's own book value is $1 per Mistee. That is the number its screens imply.

What a Mistee is worth to a player is a different question. A Mistee can only buy another spin. Ten Mistees buy one $10 open, which pays back $3.79 of pieces and 2.8 more Mistees on average, and those Mistees buy more spins, and so on. Work the loop through and one Mistee comes out at:

How you count the piecesOne Mistee is really worth
At listed pricesabout 52 cents
At resale prices, 70% of listedabout 37 cents

So when the screen credits you 2 Mistees "worth $2", you have been handed about 80 cents of spin money. Half of all opens on the Timepiece Suite end exactly like that: 1 or 2 Mistees and nothing else.

Put it together for a full open: on paper the Timepiece Suite pays back 66% of your $10. In real terms, with Mistees at 37 cents and pieces at resale, it pays back about 37%, so Misty keeps about $6.30 of every $10 before its own costs.

6b. The big prize: early, late or never

The Rolex has a chance of 0.00405% per open, 1 in 24,691. Small is not zero, and it is not a schedule. What if 10,000 opens go by and nobody has had it? What if it drops in the first 100?

Opens so farChance nobody has had the Rolex yetChance it dropped at least onceChance it dropped twice or moreRolexes expectedRolex budget collected (65 cents per open)

How the columns are made: one open misses the Rolex 99.996 times in 100; for a run of opens, multiply that miss chance by itself once per open. "At least once" is 100% minus that. "Twice or more" is 100% minus the chance of none minus the chance of exactly one (at 24,691 opens: 100% − 36.8% − 36.8% = 26.4%). "Expected" is opens × chance. The budget is opens × the Rolex's cost per open from section 4.

What Misty does about it: nothing, and that is the whole design.

  • Nothing is ever "due". The recipe has three inputs: the secret, your seed, the spin number. There is no input for "opens since the last Rolex". After 10,000 dry opens the next one is still 1 in 24,691.
  • Nothing is ever "gone". If the Rolex drops at open 100, open 101 has the same chance. There is no stock of one Rolex; Misty buys one when it is won. IcyBox-style drops and kuji boxes are different: a fixed stock, and an early win does empty the box.
  • The money side. Misty collects about 65 cents of Rolex budget per open. After 100 opens it has $65 and owes a $16,035 watch: a loss on that box for now. After about 24,700 opens the kitty crosses the cost; over many boxes and months the average holds. The margin on everything else (about $6.30 of every $10 in real terms) covers early hits. Whether Misty insures or reserves for big wins is not public.
  • What "1 in 25,000" means for a player. About one Rolex per $250,000 taken in, spread over everybody, with no pattern. Your own 500 opens have a 2% chance of containing it, whatever happened before you.
  • What we cannot see. A site could lower the chance after a big win or raise it after a drought, and every check would still pass, because the check proves the roll against the table used, not that the table is the same as yesterday's. We have no evidence Misty does this.

7. What the proof proves, and what it does not

The locked-in secret, your seed and the reveal are real, and the sum can be rerun by anyone. Together they prove three things:

  • the secret was fixed before you paid, so the result was not changed after your money went in;
  • the roll was computed from that secret, your seed and the spin number, so nobody chose it by hand;
  • the roll was read against the table that was frozen onto your open.

They do not prove:

  • that every player was given the same table (two players can each hold a valid proof against two different tables);
  • that the table today is the table of yesterday;
  • that a Mistee is worth a dollar.

The simulator shows the first gap directly. In its back office you can hand one player a private table with the pieces five times as likely, and every proof still passes for every player.

8. The back office in the simulator

The Misty page of the simulator copies the player's side exactly and adds the operator's side behind the cog.

  • Box & odds. Pick any of the thirteen boxes. Every outcome has a percentage you can change. As you type, the page shows the new sum, the chance of a piece, what the box pays back per open on paper and in real money, and what each row costs per open. One button restores the live odds; another rescales an edited table to 100.
  • What a free spin pays. The small second table, editable the same way.
  • Per player. Add players or segments (new sign-ups, VIPs, a flagged account) and give each a share of the traffic. A player either uses the box's table or their own. "Own odds" has a quick knob, pieces ×0.25 to ×5, which multiplies every piece's chance and takes the difference out of the Mistee rows so the total stays 100, and a full editor for row-by-row changes. At the top of the page you choose who is spinning.
  • What things are worth. One Mistee on screen ($1), one Mistee really (37 cents), pieces at resale (70%). All the returns on the page are computed from these.
  • The proof. Misty's recipe on, or a plain random number with nothing to check.
  • Run. Opens the box thousands of times across the players and reports: counts per outcome against the stated odds, return on paper and in real money against what the table says, each player's return against their own table, and a recomputation of the proofs from the revealed secrets.

Under the box the page writes out, in plain words, every step of the last decision: the secret locked in, the roll, the band it fell in, the free spins, what the result is worth on screen and really, and the reveal. The technical details (the scrambled secret, the seed, the long mixed number) stay folded away until you open them.

9. The short version to remember

  • Price first, odds second. The chances are tuned so the box pays back less than its price.
  • Each row costs value times chance. Add the rows and you have what the box pays back per open.
  • One open is one roll. A hundred players are a hundred independent rolls; the payout percentage only exists across all of them.
  • Nearly every open pays Mistees. They look like dollars and are worth about 40 cents.
  • The proof covers the roll, not the table and not the value.

Glossary

Odds table
The list of everything a box can pay, each with its chance. The chances add up to 100.
Band
The slice of the 0 to 100 line that belongs to one outcome; as wide as its chance.
Roll
The number between 0 and 100 that decides one open. It falls in exactly one band.
Cost per open
An outcome's value multiplied by its chance: what that outcome costs the operator on average every time the box is opened.
Pays back per open
All the costs per open added together. Divided by the price it is the return, often called RTP.
On paper
Counting Mistees at $1 and pieces at listed price, the way Misty's screens do.
In real money
Counting Mistees at what they cost the operator and pieces at resale.
Mistee
Misty's site credit. Paid by nearly every open, redeemable only for boxes and products, never for money.
Free spin
A bonus roll from a small second table that pays Mistees only.
Secret
A random number the site chooses before you pay; together with your seed it decides the roll. Revealed after the open.
Fingerprint
The scrambled form of the secret shown before you pay; the secret must match it afterwards.
Seed
A word you choose that goes into the roll alongside the secret.
Spin number
1 for the box spin, 2 and 3 for free spins, all under the same secret.
Frozen table
The odds table, stamped onto your open so the proof shows which table your roll was read against.
Back office
The operator's settings: the percentages, the free-spin table, the values, and per-player tables.
