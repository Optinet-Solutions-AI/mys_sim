3. Positioning

GrailNoir is a luxury discovery platform that tells you what the market says an item is worth, not what the operator says, and sells boxes you can audit: the list of items, their odds, their prices and the cash-out rate are locked and fingerprinted before you open, the return is shown at cash value, and you are paid in money at the published rate.

  • Category: luxury watches and bags, US first, web app.
  • Product line: "The only mystery box where you can check the box, not just the dice." Meaning: other sites let you verify that the dice roll was random (provably fair†). We also let you verify the box: what was in it, at what odds and prices, fixed before you opened it and unchanged afterwards.
  • Campaign line: "Life is too short. Go Noir." (pending trademark review)
  • Tagline: "Track the Rare. Find the Grail."

What none of IcyBox, StockX, Courtyard, Misty or the vendor's platform do, and we would:

  1. Publish the whole box (items, odds, prices, cash-out rate) as a fingerprinted file before the open.
  2. Pay cash-out in money at the price shown before the open, not store credit and not an undisclosed estimate.
  3. Show the market's value next to any operator's claimed value, for our boxes and theirs.
  4. Add a random ingredient nobody controls to every opening.
  5. Never close a finite pool early.

What this plan suggests not doing: one-in-a-million showcase items, "value" quoted at retail, credits-only cash-out, odds that sum above 100%, near-miss animations, withdrawals only "through items", undisclosed bots, expiring daily boxes, brand logos as our branding, unauthenticated goods.

5. Answers to your comments (round 1)

Numbered as in 03-plan-and-alignment-notes.md.

  1. IcyBox benchmark; web app; US; innovation. Suggested and applied in this draft (rows 1 and 3). The innovation list is §3. The second-wave audit found nobody doing items 1 to 3 on that list, so they are genuinely new.
  1. StockX and Courtyard in plain words. StockX is the big sneaker and streetwear resale marketplace. In July 2026 it launched mystery boxes with three promises: whatever you pull is worth at least what you paid for the box (by StockX's own price list), the odds are shown before you buy, and you can hand the item straight back for 75 to 90% of its value as store credit. Courtyard is a collectibles company that sells $10,000 watch "packs": the watch inside is promised to be worth between $8,000 and $90,000, and you can sell it back instantly at 95% of market value or leave it in their vault. Why it matters: two trusted brands have already made "you can't lose much and you can cash out" normal, so we cannot claim that alone as our innovation. Our edge has to be the checkable box, cash in money, and the market-value comparison.
  1. Payments. Parked (rows 4, 16, 17 marked deferred).
  1. What is Goldin. An auction house for high-end trading cards and sports memorabilia, owned by eBay. Its sold prices are what card collectors treat as the real market value. It only matters if we add collectibles later; it has no watch or bag data.
  1. Why different valuations matter. Every luxury item has three prices: what the boutique charges new (retail), what sellers ask on resale sites (asking), and what items actually sell for (realized). Mystery-box sites show the highest number as the "value" of your prize and pay you on the lowest when you cash out. That gap is the hidden profit and the reason players feel cheated. For the thesis it is the core deception mechanism. For the product it is the whole differentiator: show the real number and pay on it. If showing three numbers to users is too much, the minimum rule is "the value we show is the value we pay".
  1. More data sources, don't give up on an API. Suggested and kept open (row 8). This round's searches were rate-limited, so the next pass will cover Bezel, Subdial, WatchAnalytics, EveryWatch (sales contact), LePrix (wholesale luxury), Vestiaire and The RealReal affiliate feeds, Chrono24 dealer tools, and Google Shopping via a search API for retail prices. For research only, we can also scrape; for the product we need licences.
  1. Beacon and rotation in plain words. A box result is produced by mixing two secret ingredients: ours (the server seed) and the player's (the client seed). The weakness is that we control our ingredient and most players never change theirs, so a dishonest operator could pick favourable combinations. Fix one, the "beacon": add a third ingredient nobody controls, a public random number published every few seconds by an independent source (a blockchain block, or drand, a beacon run by a group of universities and companies), chosen only after the player clicks. Fix two, "rotation": use a fresh secret for every single opening and reveal the previous one immediately, so each open can be checked right away instead of after hundreds of plays. Some sites do one or the other; none combine both with publishing the box contents.
  1. "A pool cannot be retired early": why and from where. From IcyBox's terms of service: "Any Icebox may be terminated or removed at any time even before all Assets included in the Icebox are distributed (including any grail that had not yet been distributed)." In a finite pool the top prize is what sells the tickets. If the operator can close the pool once the cheap items are gone but before the top prize drops, the advertised odds were never real. The rule is my recommendation, not an industry rule, and it is marked "proposed".
  1. 7-day window. Moved to Open.
  1. PullValue in plain words. A small independent website that watches every pack opened on Courtyard, records what came out, and publishes "how much value you really get back" per pack, updated every ten minutes. Someone built it because buyers wanted a referee the operator does not control. That is evidence that our comparison layer, a referee for every mystery-box operator, has an audience.
  1. GGR and the vendor's process. GGR (gross gaming revenue) is total bets minus total winnings paid out, what the house keeps before bonuses and costs. NGR (net gaming revenue) is GGR minus bonuses and free boxes. The vendor charges €35,000 once plus 10% of GGR every month. The process: the operator sets up a company, licenses the software, uploads items with prices and photos, builds boxes, sets the return rate (default 90%), connects payment providers, and markets through streamers. Players deposit money, open boxes or join battles, sell items back into their balance (99.9% do), and bet again. The operator keeps 15 to 30% of what players deposited.
  1. The benchmarks in plain words. "Hold 15 to 30% of deposits": of every $100 players put in, the operator keeps $15 to $30 after prizes and bonuses. "99.9% of pulls sold back": almost nobody ships the item; they convert it to balance and keep playing. "200 orders a month": even very large operators ship about 200 physical items a month. "Lifetime value over $200": the average US player is worth more than $200 to the operator over their life. "Items listed 10% above acquisition cost": the "value" shown on the item already includes a margin, so the displayed value is not what the operator paid.
  1. "Check the box, not just the dice." Explained in §3.
  1. Independent market value: when suggested and where from. Brainstorm excerpt 3, which you pasted, states it as the strongest differentiator; dossier 2 §2.6 reached the same conclusion from the pricing research. It was recorded as a suggestion when the plan was first written because both sides favoured it.
  1. Commit the whole box manifest. Before any open, write the full box into one file: the item list, each item's odds and prices, the cash-out rate, and the secret seed. Publish the file's fingerprint (a hash: a short code that changes completely if one character changes). After the open, publish the file. Anyone can recompute the fingerprint and see it matches, which proves we did not change the box after seeing bets. The daily log fingerprint does the same for the list of all opens that day, so none can be deleted or altered. The checker is a small public program that does this verification so players do not have to trust us.
  1. Cash buyback at the published price. When a player sells an item back, we pay money (to a bank or wallet), not store credit, at the market price we published for that item before the open. StockX pays store credit that expires. IcyBox pays an "estimated value" it refuses to explain. Jemlit and Misty pay credits that can never leave.
  1. Purpose of three prices and a confidence band. See answer 5. The confidence band says how sure we are of the market price, based on how many recent sales exist: a Rolex Submariner has hundreds, a rare Hermès colour has three.
  1. "Comparison layer ships before boxes." A website, with no boxes of our own yet, that lists items offered by existing mystery-box sites, shows each operator's claimed value next to the real market value, and tracks live drops. First because it needs no inventory, no payments and no gambling-law exposure, and it builds our price database and audience. "First" is about order, not a date; we are in research.
  1. Entropy, seeds, beacon, drand, rotation. See answer 7 and the glossary. "Entropy" just means genuine unpredictability.
  1. 7-day window. Open, as you asked.
  1. Committed pools. See answer 8.

Asking price
What a seller lists an item for. Often 25% or more above what it finally sells for.
Battle
Several players open the same boxes; the one whose pulls add up to the most takes everything.
Beacon (random beacon)
A public source of random numbers that nobody controls, published on a schedule.
Buyback
The operator's offer to buy your pulled item back for balance or money.
Client seed
The player's secret ingredient in the randomness. Usually set by the site's own code unless
Collectibles
Trading cards, graded cards, memorabilia. Goldin and Courtyard live here.
Comparison layer
A referee site: each operator's claimed value next to the market's value, for every item.
Comps (comparable sales)
Recent real sales of the same item, used to estimate what the next one is worth.
Confidence band
How sure a price estimate is, from the number of recent sales behind it.
Entropy
Genuine unpredictability in a random process.
Expected value (EV)
The average payout of a box over a huge number of opens.
Fingerprint (hash)
A short code computed from a file; any change to the file changes the code completely.
Floor (value floor)
A promise that whatever you pull is worth at least some amount, usually the box price.
GGR / NGR
Gross gaming revenue is bets minus winnings; net gaming revenue also subtracts bonuses.
Hold
The share of deposited money the operator keeps.
Lifetime value (LTV)
Average revenue from one player over the whole relationship.
Manifest
The single file describing a box completely: items, odds, prices, cash-out rate and seed.
Pool (finite pool, kuji)
A box backed by a fixed inventory; odds equal the remaining items and change as
Provably fair
A scheme that lets a player check after the fact that the dice roll was fixed before the bet.
Realized price
What an item actually sold for: the only price that is money.
Retail price
What the boutique charges new.
RTP (return to player)
Expected value as a percentage of the price. 90% means $90 back per $100 bet on average.
Seed rotation
Using a fresh server seed for each opening and revealing the previous one.
Server seed
The operator's secret ingredient in the randomness, committed by its fingerprint before play.
PullValue
A third-party tracker of real returns on Courtyard packs.
